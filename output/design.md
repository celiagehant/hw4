# Campus Customs — Storefront Redesign

The redesign turns a plain product list into a premium Yale storefront. Each change below says what we did and why it should keep shoppers on the site and lead them to buy.

## 1. Brand look: type and color

**What:**
- Two fonts. Cormorant Garamond, an elegant serif, is used for headlines and the "Campus Customs" wordmark. Inter, a clean sans-serif, is used for prices, buttons and text.
- A warm off-white background, dark navy text, Yale blue for buttons and prices, and one muted gold accent for highlights.

**Why it helps:**
- Shoppers judge quality in seconds, and a site that looks like a premium university bookstore earns the trust needed to spend $45–$98 on a sweatshirt.
- Yale blue appears only on things you can act on (buttons, prices, the selected size), so the eye goes straight to the next step.
- All text colors pass accessibility contrast standards, so everyone can read prices and buttons.

## 2. Header

**What:**
- A blue strip reading "Official Yale University merchandise · 57 Broadway, New Haven".
- A "CC" monogram and the store name.
- A cart button with a live count that pops when something is added.
- A clear "Hi, Name" when logged in.
- On scroll, the header slims down and blurs; on phones, the links move into a menu button.

**Why it helps:**
- "Official" and the real store address sit at the top of every page, which builds trust before anything else.
- The cart is always one tap away and confirms each add, so shoppers keep adding instead of wondering whether it worked.
- The slimmer header leaves more room for products.

## 3. Home page that sells

**What:**
- A hero reading "Your Yale. *Your Style.*", with two buttons: "Shop the Collection" and "Ask our AI stylist".
- A collage of real product photos with a gold "Hoodies from $45.00" tag.
- A featured collection (one large product, two smaller ones), category tiles showing the number of styles and the "from" price, and a "Meet Dan" section with ready-made questions.

**Why it helps:**
- Visitors see real products and a starting price immediately, not just a welcome message, and there is a clear next click for both browsers and shoppers who want help.
- Category tiles ("Hoodies · 27 styles · from $45.00") answer "do they have what I want, and can I afford it?" in one glance.
- Every product, price and count comes live from the database, so the page is never out of date.

## 4. Product cards

**What:**
- Large square photos, product type, name, price, a short description and the sizes in stock.
- "Low stock" and "Sold out" badges.
- On hover the photo zooms gently, the card lifts, and Add to Cart turns blue.

**Why it helps:**
- Shoppers can compare price and size availability without opening each product.
- "Low stock" adds honest urgency.
- The hover effects make the grid feel responsive and invite a click, and Add to Cart is right on the card, so buying takes fewer steps.

## 5. Product page

**What:**
- A two-column layout: a large photo on the left, purchase details on the right.
- The selected size turns blue, gets a gold ✓ and is labelled "Selected: XL".
- Live stock messages ("Only 2 left in XL."), a quantity control, and a full-width Add to Cart button.
- An "Ask Dan" card, and a short facts list (type, colors, sizes in stock). On phones, everything stacks.

**Why it helps:**
- Everything needed to decide is visible next to the photo, in the order people decide: what is it, how much, my size, add.
- The clear size state and stock messages prevent ordering mistakes and returns.
- Dan is one click away for questions that would otherwise make a shopper leave.

## 6. Dan, the AI shopping companion

**What:**
- The chat became "Dan", with a friendly bulldog avatar inspired by Handsome Dan, Yale's mascot.
- A welcome message with suggested questions: "Find me a Yale hoodie", "What's under $50?", "Help me find a gift", "Show me what's available in medium".
- Typing dots while he answers, and product cards inside the chat with a "Why Dan picked it" reason, View Product and Add to Cart.
- On a product page, Dan shows "Helping with: \<product\>" and offers questions about it.

**Why it helps:**
- A named mascot feels like a helpful shop assistant, not a support bot, so more people start a conversation.
- The suggested questions remove the "what do I type?" hurdle.
- Shoppers can go from question to cart without leaving the chat.
- Showing the product Dan is helping with builds confidence that his answer is about the right item.

## 7. Motion

**What:** short, smooth animations everywhere: pages fade in, cards lift, the cart count pops, and chat messages and recommendations slide in. Nothing moves constantly except the typing dots while Dan is answering, and all motion turns off for visitors whose device asks for reduced motion.

**Why it helps:** motion confirms actions (added, sent, loaded) and makes the site feel polished, without slowing anyone down or distracting from products.

## 8. Works on every screen, for everyone

**What:**
- Phones get a menu button, a two-column product grid, a stacked product page and the chat as a panel from the bottom.
- Everyone gets a "Skip to content" link, visible keyboard focus, alt text on every image, and labels on every button.

**Why it helps:**
- Many shoppers, especially students and visiting families, browse on their phones, so a store that works well there keeps those sales.
- Accessible design means no customer is shut out of buying.

## The result

The site now feels like a store, guides shoppers to their next step on every page, and offers a friendly assistant when they hesitate. Every product, price and stock figure still comes from the store's database, so the polish never comes at the cost of accuracy.
